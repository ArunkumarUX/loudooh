First, the numbers that actually matter

Buses carried around 3.4 billion passenger journeys in England alone in the year to March 2025, according to Department for Transport data, with London accounting for roughly half of that. Bus remains the most used form of public transport in the UK by a wide margin - more journeys than the entire national rail network and the London Underground combined.

For advertisers, passenger numbers are only half the story. A bus advert is seen mostly by people who are not on the bus.

Street-level

Goes where billboards cannot

There are streets in every UK town where no billboard exists and none ever will - and a bus route runs down them every ten minutes. Bus is the only large-format OOH channel that penetrates high streets, residential areas, and town centres with no other inventory at all.

Fixed routes

Repetition built in

A bus runs the same roads all day, every day. People who live, work, or shop along that route see the same campaign again and again for the full booking period - frequency at street level, across the whole route rather than at fixed points.

Industry estimates put exterior impressions for a single urban bus at 30,000 to 50,000 per week on busy city routes.

Who actually sees bus advertising? The audience question.

Bus advertising has two audiences, and they are very different sizes. Understanding which formats reach which is fundamental to whether a campaign works.

Whole route

Exterior audience

Mass local · every day

Everyone the bus passes: pedestrians, drivers at lights, people at stops, shoppers, school runs, office workers. It does not target a demographic - it targets a geography. Route selection is the targeting mechanism.

~20 min

Interior audience

Passengers · long dwell

Around 3.4 billion journeys a year in England. Passengers skew younger adults, students, and older residents - locally rooted. Panels sit in their sightline for the whole journey.

Both

Best campaigns run both

Reach + reading time

Exteriors for reach and brand presence across a geography. Interiors for messages that need reading time - local services, recruitment, QR codes. The second audience comes cheap once you are paying for the first.

The practical rule

Exteriors are for reach and brand presence across a geography. Interiors are for messages that need reading time. The best bus campaigns usually run both on the same fleet, because the interior audience comes cheap once you are paying for the exterior.

What a real campaign looks like at different budget levels

Bus advertising is a fleet medium: single-bus campaigns exist but the format works through repetition across routes. Here is what different budgets genuinely buy on a standard two-week cycle (supersides and rears). Mega rears and regional wraps are annual commitments - plan those separately from short-cycle fleets.

Under £2,500

Local

Genuine local presence

Ten to fifteen regional supersides or rears for two weeks, concentrated on the one or two routes that pass through your catchment (regional supersides start from £130 per bus, minimum 10). One of the most accessible entry points in all of UK outdoor. Add interior panels by route or line once you are buying the exterior, and you own both audiences on your local routes.

£2.5k – £10k

Town / city

Town or city coverage

Twenty to forty buses across a town's main routes, or a mixed-format campaign combining T-sides for impact with rears for the driver audience. Local people start to feel the brand is everywhere - exactly the effect bus advertising is best at creating.

£10k – £30k

Scale

City dominance or multi-town

Seventy-five to 150 buses across a major city, or coordinated fleets across several towns. Mix T-sides and Streetliners where the fleet is single-decker, and add interior panels fleet-wide where the message needs reading time. Typical territory for regional consumer brands, property developers, universities, and healthcare.

£30k+

Landmark

Wraps and landmark campaigns

London full wraps run £10,000–£30,000 per bus per four weeks; regional wraps start from £18,000 per bus per year. At this level you are buying landmark presence - multi-city coverage, or full-format domination of a single city's network - and wrapped double deckers reliably earn social and press coverage on top of paid exposure.

The factors that move the price

Five variables explain most of the difference between two otherwise similar bus campaigns.

01

Biggest lever

London versus regional

London bus advertising prices at roughly double the regional equivalent, reflecting TfL network scale, audience density, and demand. Regional and town fleets are where the value sits for most SMEs: audiences are locally concentrated and pricing falls faster than the impressions do.

02

Volume

Fleet size and volume discounts

Per-bus rates fall as fleet size rises, typically stepping down at around 20, 50, and 100 buses. A 150-bus campaign costs materially less per bus than a 10-bus campaign. Committing to volume upfront always beats building incrementally at open rates.

03

Package

Format mix

Rears, sides, and interiors are priced separately but bundle well. Running multiple formats on the same fleet through one booking is consistently cheaper than booking them separately - and means the same bus works the pavement, the traffic behind it, and the passengers inside it simultaneously.

04

Timing

Campaign duration

Two weeks is the standard period. Because route repetition is the mechanism that makes bus work, longer campaigns outperform disproportionately. Four, eight and twelve-week bookings reduce the per-period rate and give the frequency effect time to compound.

05

Total cost

Production

Printed vinyl for rears and sides typically adds £40 to £90 per panel including fitting. Full wraps need specialist installation, often charged separately. Interior panels print cheaply. Digital formats, where available, carry no production cost. Build production into the total from the start.

Production rule: Always build print and fitting into the total budget from the start - especially on wrap campaigns where production is a major line.

Does it work? The honest answer on effectiveness.

Bus advertising works through repetition and proximity. Its effectiveness case rests on three things that are genuinely true - and one limitation that matters.

✓

Yes - if you need geographic saturation at street level

Bus is the best value geographic saturation tool in UK outdoor. It rewards brands with a defined local or city catchment, simple bold creative, and the patience to run two weeks or longer.

Street-level presence where nothing else reaches

Bus is the only large-format channel present on most UK high streets and residential corridors. Local recall studies consistently show strong prompted awareness for bus campaigns among residents of the routes covered, precisely because the exposure repeats daily for a month or more.

The captive driver

The rear of a bus is one of the most reliably viewed surfaces in British life. Drivers stuck behind a bus have nowhere else to look, repeatedly, for the length of every queue - short-copy territory for phone numbers, web addresses, and single-line offers.

Interior dwell at accessible prices

Twenty minutes of passenger dwell, priced by route or line and often bundled with an exterior buy, is the quiet bargain of UK outdoor. Recruitment, colleges, local health services, and anything QR-code-led perform well here, with response directly measurable.

Where it's strongest

Geographic saturation at street level, captive rear exposure for drivers, and long-dwell interiors priced by route - especially for brands with a defined local or city catchment and bold, brief creative.

What bus advertising does not do well

Exterior exposure is brief and moving - creative that needs study time dies on a superside. The audience is broad rather than targeted, so premium and niche B2B brands will find less concentration than on the Underground or at airports. Impression figures are modelled estimates - treat precise-sounding numbers as directional. For verified measurement, Route data is the industry standard.

Bus advertising rewards brands with a defined local or city catchment, simple bold creative, and the patience to run two weeks or longer. It punishes complicated messages and scattergun route selection.

Choosing routes and cities: a practical framework

Match the routes to your catchment - not just the biggest fleet number.

Local businesses: buy your own routes

Identify the two or three routes that pass your premises or cover your customer catchment, and concentrate everything there. A gym, dental practice, or restaurant advertising on the routes its customers ride and walk along every day is buying the most relevant impressions available at any price.

City campaigns: cover the corridors

Every city has main bus corridors where dozens of routes converge on the centre. Fleet selection weighted to these corridors puts your campaign on the busiest streets in town all day long. Corridor coverage plus a spread across residential routes gives both intensity and reach.

Regional campaigns: town by town

Outside the big cities, bus advertising is often the only OOH channel with real presence in market towns and suburbs. Regional brands can build coverage town by town at regional rates, reaching communities that national media plans routinely miss entirely.

London: zones and boroughs

The London network is vast, and campaigns are planned by area - routes serving particular boroughs, corridors, or the West End and City. One of the more affordable ways to buy visible, moving presence in the capital, sitting well below Underground entry costs while covering ground the Tube does not.

The booking process: what actually happens

Allow three to four weeks from brief to buses on the road for standard printed formats. Artwork approval takes a few days, print and fitting one to two weeks, and buses are fitted as they cycle through the depot - so a large fleet reaches full coverage over several days rather than overnight. Full wraps need four to six weeks minimum. Interior campaigns can move in two to three weeks.

Week 1 - Brief & route plan

Route selection, format mix, fleet size, and rate confirmation.

Week 2 - Artwork

Creative sign-off, CAP compliance, and vehicle template specs (including rear furniture).

Weeks 3–4 - Production & live

Print and depot fitting; fleet coverage builds as buses cycle through.

UK bus advertising inventory is controlled by media owners holding contracts with the bus operators, and different owners cover different operators and regions. A multi-city campaign usually means multiple owners - booking through an agency consolidates the plan, the paperwork, and the pricing leverage into one place.

Compliance: All creative must comply with the CAP Code. Bus formats carry specific rules: nothing that could be mistaken for road signage, restrictions on certain categories, and operator-level approval on all artwork. Rear designs must work around number plates and light clusters - build to the actual template from the start. Submit artwork at least five working days before your fitting date.

Is bus advertising right for an SME?

Straight answer: Bus advertising is arguably the most SME-friendly channel in UK outdoor. Entry pricing starts in the hundreds, the targeting mechanism is geography rather than expensive data, and the format reaches exactly the local audiences most small businesses live on.

When it makes sense

  • Your customers are concentrated in a specific town, city, or set of neighbourhoods
  • You want repeated local exposure rather than one-off reach
  • Your brand benefits from feeling established and visible in its own community
  • You have a simple, bold message or a strong visual identity
  • You can commit to two weeks or longer so the route repetition compounds

When to think twice

  • Your audience is a narrow professional or premium segment better concentrated elsewhere
  • Your message needs study time and you are only buying exteriors
  • You need precisely measured, trackable response as the primary metric
  • Your catchment does not align with any meaningful route pattern
  • Your creative budget cannot stretch to work that reads at a glance from a moving vehicle

How to get more from your bus advertising budget

Ten buses on the two routes your customers actually use beat thirty buses scattered across a city.

Five planning moves that consistently improve what a bus budget delivers.

01

Routes

Concentrate on routes, not coverage maps

Frequency with the right people is the mechanism. Choose routes the way you would choose shop premises: by who walks past.

02

Creative

Design for three seconds and thirty metres

Exterior bus creative is read at a glance from across the street or through a windscreen. One visual, one message, legible branding, and contrast that survives a grey February afternoon. If the design needs explaining, it needs redesigning.

03

Response

Put the call to action on the rear

Drivers behind the bus have time to read and remember. Sides build the brand; the rear closes. Phone number, web address, or offer on the rear, brand story on the sides, is the classic split for good reason.

04

Interior

Add interiors whenever the budget allows

Interior panels are priced by route or line, cost a fraction of exteriors when bundled, and deliver twenty minutes of dwell to a locally rooted audience. For any campaign with detail to communicate, the interior add-on is the cheapest incremental audience in the plan.

05

Measurement

Run search alongside, as always

Bus campaigns lift local branded search the same way every awareness format does. Paid search on your brand and category terms during the campaign captures the interest the buses create, and Google Search Console gives you the cleanest free read on whether the campaign is registering locally.

Final thoughts

Bus advertising is the street-level workhorse of UK outdoor. It goes where no other large format exists, repeats along its routes every day for the length of the booking, and prices in at the most accessible entry level of any channel we have covered in this series. It is not glamorous, and its impressions are estimates rather than certainties, but pound for pound it saturates a local geography better than anything else you can buy.

The campaigns that win choose routes like premises, keep the creative bold and brief, put the call to action on the rear, add interiors by route when the message needs reading time, and run long enough for the repetition to do its work.
The campaigns that disappoint buy scattered buses with complicated artwork for a single short burst and conclude the medium does not work. It does. It just rewards discipline.
