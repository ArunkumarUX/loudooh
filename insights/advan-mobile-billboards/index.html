I've run enough AdVan and mobile billboard campaigns to know that the biggest mistake is treating them like a billboard that happens to have wheels.

The whole point of mobile billboard advertising is that you can take the campaign to the audience.

You can put a branded vehicle outside London's advertising agencies, move through a busy shopping district, target a specific postcode, build a route around an event or change the campaign geography at different times of the day.

That changes how you plan the media. With a traditional billboard, you buy the site. With an AdVan, you are buying the combination of vehicle, route, timing and audience. Get those four things right and a focused mobile campaign can create a level of visibility that is difficult to achieve through a fixed site alone.

I've seen this work particularly well for brands with a very clear audience or a very specific reason for being somewhere. It is why I think mobile billboards are particularly interesting for B2B campaigns, challenger brands, product launches, retail and events.

There is also a lot of nonsense written about this part of the OOH market. You will often see huge audience numbers with little explanation, or a headline price that looks cheap until you find out what is actually included.

So this guide is about the practical side of AdVan and mobile billboard advertising: what it costs, the different vehicle options, where I would use them, what different budgets can realistically buy and when mobile OOH makes more sense than a fixed billboard.

These are the questions I would ask before putting a campaign on the road.

The three core mobile billboard formats

The three core formats in this guide are Standard, Electric and XL. The Standard and Electric both provide a 20sqm advertising surface, while the XL increases the advertising area to 35sqm.

The format should follow the campaign rather than the other way around. I would start with the audience and objective, then decide how much physical presence is actually needed.

Core format

Standard AdVan | 20sqm

The Standard is the format I would normally look at first when the brief is about targeted mobile visibility rather than maximum physical scale. At 20sqm, there is enough advertising surface to create a strong presence while keeping the campaign flexible. I would keep the creative simple. The vehicle may be seen for only a few seconds, so large branding, a strong visual and one clear message will normally work harder than a wall of copy.

The Electric format keeps the 20sqm advertising footprint but uses an electric vehicle platform. I see this as a premium option rather than simply another version of the Standard. It is particularly relevant when the vehicle itself supports the brand positioning or when the campaign is operating in an urban environment where a premium electric platform makes sense. I would still start with the audience. Choosing an electric vehicle should support the campaign strategy, not replace it. Any environmental claims should be specific and properly evidenced.

XL mobile billboard | 35sqm

The XL is the format I would use when physical presence is the priority. At 35sqm, it gives the creative a much larger canvas and is particularly suited to major launches, entertainment, retail, automotive and campaigns where the vehicle needs to be noticed from further away. But bigger is not automatically better. If the audience is tightly concentrated, a well-routed 20sqm vehicle can be a smarter buy than a larger vehicle travelling broadly.

Why mobile billboards work

I think mobile OOH is at its best when there is a reason for the vehicle to be somewhere.

That could be a business district full of the people you want to reach, a retail catchment, a competitor location, a product launch, an exhibition, a sporting event or a particular city-centre audience.

The route becomes part of the media plan. That is the big difference between a fixed billboard and a mobile billboard.

Mobile billboards vs traditional billboards

A traditional billboard is fixed. That is a strength when you want consistent visibility from the same audience over a long period. Mobile billboards give you something different: the geography can change, the route can be built around a specific audience and the campaign can concentrate activity around a particular time or event. I would choose the fixed site when sustained location-based presence is the priority. I would choose mobile OOH when timing, geography or audience concentration is important. Sometimes the strongest campaign uses both.

B2B, events and competitor targeting

Three situations where a mobile billboard becomes a very targeted piece of OOH rather than just another awareness channel.

B2B and agency targeting

This is one of the areas where I think mobile billboard advertising is particularly underused. London has dense clusters of media agencies, creative agencies, technology businesses and professional services firms. If the audience is the people working in those businesses, I would rather build a route around the relevant office clusters than buy broad London coverage simply because it looks impressive on a media plan.

Events and launches

Events create temporary concentrations of people, which makes them a natural fit for mobile OOH. I would look at the whole journey, not just the venue. Where are people travelling from? Which stations and transport hubs will they use? Where are the hotels and surrounding entertainment areas? The route can then be built around those movements before, during and after the event.

Competitor targeting

One of the more creative uses of a mobile billboard is geographical competitor targeting. A vehicle can be routed around competitor offices, stores or business districts. For challenger brands, that can create a very direct physical presence without requiring a huge market-wide budget. I would still make sure the route has a genuine audience rationale. Driving past a competitor once is a stunt. Building a repeatable route around the relevant audience is media planning.

How I would plan the route

I would never start by asking where the vehicle can go. I would start by asking where the audience is.

Then I would split the day into useful windows. Morning business activity can be very different from lunchtime retail activity or evening entertainment.

Recruitment campaigns

A recruitment campaign might need business districts and universities.

Retail campaigns

A retail campaign might need shopping areas and transport hubs.

B2B campaigns

A B2B campaign might need office clusters.

Event campaigns

An event campaign needs the venue and the routes people take to get there.

The route should follow the brief, not the other way around.

What makes the creative work?

Mobile billboard creative needs to be understood quickly.

I would keep the brand large, the message short and the visual strong. If there is a URL, QR code or call to action, it needs to be genuinely usable from the environment in which the vehicle is being seen.

The biggest mistake is treating the side of a vehicle like a website. It is not. The vehicle is the media environment and the creative has to work at speed, at distance and from different viewing angles.

How do you measure mobile OOH?

I would look beyond clicks.

Useful measures include estimated audience, impacts, route completion, GPS reporting, photographic proof, geographic delivery, frequency, website traffic, branded search, QR scans, campaign-specific landing pages, promo codes, leads and social activity.

MeasureWhat it tells you
Estimated audience & impacts The modelled scale of who saw the vehicle on its route
Route completion & GPS reporting Proof of where the vehicle actually travelled
Photographic proof Visual evidence of the campaign in the planned environment
Geographic delivery & frequency Whether activity concentrated where the audience is
Website traffic & branded search Uplift in direct and search interest during the campaign
QR scans, landing pages & promo codes Direct response attribution where the creative carries a call to action
Leads & social activity Commercial outcomes and earned pickup

For an awareness campaign, I would not expect every person who sees the vehicle to scan a QR code. The job of OOH is often to build memory and salience first, with search, social, PR and other channels picking up the response.

Is mobile billboard advertising right for an SME?

Straight answer: Yes, provided the campaign is focused.

I would not tell a small business to spend its entire budget driving around an entire city. I would define the audience, identify the strongest locations and build the route around them.

That can make a relatively modest budget feel much bigger because the campaign is concentrated rather than diluted.

Final thoughts

Mobile billboards are not a gimmick. They are a practical OOH format when the campaign has a clear reason to be somewhere.

The biggest advantage is not simply that the billboard moves. It is that the media plan can move with the audience.

Start with the audience. Then the objective. Then the geography. Then the vehicle. Then the creative.

That is how I would plan a mobile billboard campaign, and it is how I think brands get much more from mobile OOH.
